\section{The centered difference}
Let $u$ have six continuous derivatives near $x$ and let $h>0$.
We define the centered second difference by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stencil}
 D_h u(x)=\frac{u(x+h)-2u(x)+u(x-h)}{h^2}.
\end{equation}
Taylor expansion at $x$ gives
\begin{align*}
 u(x\mathbin{\pm}h)
 &=u(x)\mathbin{\pm}hu'(x)+\frac{h^2}{2}u''(x)
   \mathbin{\pm}\frac{h^3}{6}u^{(3)}(x)
   +\frac{h^4}{24}u^{(4)}(x)
   \mathbin{\pm}\frac{h^5}{120}u^{(5)}(x)+O(h^6).
\end{align*}
Adding the two expansions and subtracting $2u(x)$ cancels the odd
powers. Dividing by $h^2$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:error}
 D_h u(x)=u''(x)+\frac{h^2}{12}u^{(4)}(x)+O(h^4).
\end{equation}
The leading error is second order in $h$.

\section{A polynomial check}
For $u(x)=x^4$ at $x=1$ with $h=1/2$, direct substitution gives
\begin{align*}
 D_hu(1)&=\frac{(3/2)^4-2+(1/2)^4}{(1/2)^2}=\frac{25}{2},\\
 u''(1)&=12,\qquad
 \frac{h^2}{12}u^{(4)}(1)=\frac{(1/2)^2}{12}\,24=\frac{1}{2}.
\end{align*}
Thus $D_hu(1)-u''(1)=1/2$, exactly the leading error term.
The remainder vanishes here because $u$ is a quartic polynomial.
